Merged research on dead/abandoned history newsletters, apps/sites, and failed self-published history-adjacent books, with repeating failure patterns and what they mean for a solo agent-run book operation.

Dead competitors — history newsletters, apps, and self-published books

Research date: 2026-08-27. 21 verified cases across 3 segments. Method: WebSearch (Wayback/rb browser tool not used in this pass — see docs/notes/source-access.md for that gap).

Failure patterns that repeat across all cases

Ranked by how often they show up, most common first.

1. No audience built before launch (the single biggest pattern)

The product went out into the world with no one waiting for it. Sales or readers depended on luck, not a built list.

  • KDP Case 1 (u/InterestingTwo8788): $35 earned, no built audience.
  • KDP Case 3 (u/hajorac): zero sales in 2 months, no marketing.
  • KDP Case 4 (u/PhantomDiclonius): wrong audience (gamers, not book buyers).
  • KDP Case 5 (u/Leanaul1998): "Pay-to-Play... unless you are already famous."
  • KDP Case 7 (J. A. Roberts): "I put my book out with NO audience."
  • The Herodotus History Blog: no subscriber signal found at all, ever.
  • The Appendix, History Is Now Magazine: no subscriber counts found in any source — a sign none were tracked or built as a real asset.

2. Free or ad-supported, funded by someone else who can pull the plug

The product never charged its own users. It ran on a platform's program, an investor round, or a parent company's budget. When that funder changed its mind, the product died overnight, regardless of reader demand.

  • Timeline (Medium): killed when Medium ended its paid publisher program.
  • Curiosity.com: killed by Discovery's own portfolio decision, not by users leaving (5M+ downloads, 4.6 stars).
  • Great Big Story: CNN/WarnerMedia pulled $70M in funding; "no path to grow it financially."
  • OZY Media: investor-funded model, killed by a fraud exposé.
  • Seeker: killed by a corporate merger (Vox absorbing Group Nine).
  • Dipity: seed-funded, ran out of runway with no follow-on round.
  • ChronoZoom: Microsoft Research retired sponsorship once its research goal was met.

3. Solo operator burnout, then silent abandonment

One person ran the whole thing. No stated reason, no succession plan. It just stopped.

  • Reading the Western Canon: 12-14 hours per essay, explicit burnout post.
  • The Herodotus History Blog: stopped posting in 2018, no farewell note.
  • The Appendix: stopped after 8 issues, no postmortem found.
  • History Is Now Magazine: "paused" notice has sat unchanged for over a decade.
  • Histography.io: SSL certificate expired, no one renewed it. A design portfolio piece, never a business.

4. Marketing was one channel, run once

Cases that did try to market did the minimum: a single Facebook post to friends, one small ad budget, no repeat effort.

  • KDP Case 1, 3, 4, 6, 7: friends/family Facebook post was the whole campaign, or a single small ad buy ($50) with no follow-up.

5. Cover and pricing did not match genre expectations

Readers judged the book before the blurb. A cover or price that reads wrong for the category kills conversion even when the book itself is fine.

  • KDP Case 2: covers "don't reflect the genre" across 5 books.
  • KDP Case 3: cover reads as fantasy, book is historical romance; priced at £4.99-£17.99 for a debut with zero reviews.
  • KDP Case 6 (book 1): no ARC, no marketing, book 2 did worse.

Cases

Newsletters (5 verified)

Timeline (timeline.com / Medium) — narrative US history for a general reader, free, Facebook-driven traffic. Ran 2014/16-2018. Died July 2018 when Medium shut its two-year paid publisher program; team was laid off. Domain now redirects to an unrelated supplement brand. Confidence: strong (named reporting, TechCrunch, Medium post by the CEO).

History Is Now Magazine (historyisnowmagazine.com) — free digital magazine of general-reader essays on modern history. Last issue August 2014. Site still carries a "paused" notice over a decade later. Cause of death: not stated by the team. Confidence: death is solid, cause is unknown.

The Appendix (theappendix.net) — free quarterly magazine mixing academic and general-reader history writing. Ran 2012-2015, 8 issues, then stopped. Cause of death: not stated. Confidence: death is solid, cause is unknown.

Reading the Western Canon (Substack, Joe Steakley) — paid-supported essays on classic texts for general readers. Borderline fit for "history" but included for a strong burnout account. Ran about a year (2023-2024). Author announced hiatus, citing 12-14 hours per essay and no routine that fit alongside other obligations. Confidence: strong (author's own words), but this is a hiatus, not a confirmed permanent end.

The Herodotus History Blog (WordPress) — free hobbyist blog on general history topics. Last post March 2018, still live but frozen, no farewell note. Cause of death: not stated, inferred from silence. Confidence: death is solid, cause is unverified.

Apps & sites (8 verified)

Curiosity.com — free daily explainer articles/videos, science and history. 5M+ downloads, 4.6-star rating. App pulled from Google Play March 2020. Cause: Discovery folded it into Discovery.com and moved investment to a podcast instead. Confidence: strong (Discovery's own shutdown notice).

Dipity — free interactive timeline-builder used by history teachers. Raised ~$900K seed. Went offline around September 2016; domain later repurposed for unrelated content. Cause: ran out of funding, no follow-on round, users lost work with no migration path. Confidence: strong (multiple independent user accounts).

Great Big Story (CNN/WarnerMedia) — short-form documentary video, 14M+ social followers, Emmy winner. Shut down September 2020. Cause: CNN president stated directly there was "no path to grow it financially," compounded by COVID ad recession and AT&T cost-cutting. Confidence: strong (named executive statement, multiple trade press).

BackStory with the American History Guys (podcast) — 12 years, 300+ episodes, millions of listeners. Final episode around July 2021. Cause: planned, deliberate ending, not a forced shutdown. Confidence: strong.

OZY Media — free general-knowledge media brand including history content. Raised $70-85M, claimed a $159M valuation. Ceased operations October 2021. Cause: confirmed investor fraud (COO impersonated a YouTube executive to secure a $40M investment; SEC charged the company and CEO). Confidence: strong (SEC case, NPR/Fortune reporting).

Seeker (Discovery/Group Nine/Vox) — explainer video, ~4,000-video library. Stopped publishing new videos in early 2023. Cause: absorbed into The Verge after Vox Media's acquisition of Group Nine and subsequent layoffs. Confidence: strong (Vox's own account, trade press).

ChronoZoom (Microsoft Research) — free zoomable "Big History" timeline. Now labeled "retired" by Microsoft Research; code archived on GitHub. Cause: institutional research project wound down once its goals were met, never a commercial product. Confidence: strong (Microsoft's own project page).

Histography.io — free single-page interactive world-history timeline, a personal design project. SSL certificate expired September 2024, traffic collapsed. Cause: no revenue model once press attention faded; no one to pay for upkeep. Confidence: weak — the site still technically loads, so this is dormant, not confirmed dead.

Self-published books, history-adjacent (8 verified, first-hand accounts)

Note: history-specific first-hand KDP post-mortems are rare. Most cases below are general fiction/nonfiction, but the economics and causes transfer directly to a solo nonfiction history book.

  1. u/InterestingTwo8788 — debut book, spent ~$400 on cover/ARC/ copyright/hosting, earned $35. Cause: no audience, rushed edit.
  2. u/VLK249 — 5 self-published books, all under the author's own 250-sale "flop" line. Cause: mismatched covers/genres across pen name, no niche, no email list, no ad spend.
  3. u/hajorac — historical-romance-adjacent story collection, zero sales in 2 months. Cause: cover read as the wrong genre, overpriced for a debut, zero reviews, no marketing.
  4. u/PhantomDiclonius — debut novel, $50 in Amazon Ads, 100,000+ impressions, 74 clicks, zero sales. Cause: existing audience (gaming subscribers) did not convert to book buyers.
  5. u/Leanaul1998 — niche literary fantasy, 30 copies sold in 2 months. Cause, in the author's words: "Pay-To-Play... unless you are already famous." NetGalley ARC spend called a waste.
  6. u/Sunshinebooks8 + thread — cozy fantasy, book 2 sold worse than book 1 (no ARC or marketing on book 1). A separate commenter's book sold ~20 copies in 3 years, mostly to family. Cause: releasing book 2 does not fix a book 1 discoverability problem.
  7. J. A. Roberts ("History on a Whim," history-themed pen name) — 7 sales in the first month, 3 from a single Facebook post. Cause, in the author's words: "I put my book out into the world with NO audience."
  8. u/Sausage_fingies — debut poetry, 12 copies in week 1, framed by the author as a small win. Included only as a calibration point for what "low" looks like, not a full post-mortem.

What this means for us

  • Do not build on borrowed ground. Free platforms and ad-funded models die when someone else's budget changes, not when readers stop wanting the thing. Sell direct (Amazon KDP purchase, own list) so our survival depends on buyers, not a platform's mood.
  • Build the list before the launch, not after. Every failed book case had no audience waiting on day one. Even a small list of 50 real people beats zero every time.
  • One book, one push, then silence kills. A single Facebook post or a $50 ad buy is not a campaign. Budget for repeat, small, cheap pushes instead of one big one.
  • Match the cover and price to the genre on sight. Readers judge in under a second. A history book needs to look like a history book a stranger would trust, priced low ($0.99-2.99) for the first release from an unknown name.
  • Plan for the solo-operator failure mode now. Every blog/newsletter death in this research was one person who quietly stopped, with 3-5 hours a week and no backup. Keep the process light enough to survive a bad month without vanishing.
  • Series beats singles. A lone book with no backlist has nothing to compound. Plan the second and third title before the first launch, so a slow first sale still has somewhere to lead.
