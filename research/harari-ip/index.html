Can we use Harari's style, quote him, and namedrop Sapiens?

Not legal advice. This is open-web research for a one-person publisher, written to be actionable, not a legal opinion. If any of this becomes a real dispute (a takedown, a cease-and-desist), get an actual IP lawyer — ideally one in Italy who also knows US copyright, since we sell into both.

VERDICT

1. Writing in Harari's style/method (pendulum, reframe, thesis-first, compression) → YES. Structure, technique, and method are not protectable, in the US or in Italy/EU. Use the whole toolkit in docs/house-style.md freely. The only line: don't lift his actual sentences, invented terms as sentences, or paragraph-level phrasing. Don't reuse "imagined order" as our own coined term dressed up as if it were ours — not because it's illegal (a two-word phrase isn't copyrightable either), but because it reads as derivative, which is a brand risk, not a legal one.

2. Short verbatim Sapiens quotes as an Instagram hook → CONDITIONAL, lean NO for repeat commercial use. A single short quote used once might survive as US fair use or Italian/EU "quotation," but an ongoing marketing campaign built on Harari's verbatim words is the fact pattern that fails both tests: the use is commercial, repeated, not criticism/review, and its whole purpose is to borrow his voice to sell our product — that is close to the "heart of the use" both regimes exist to stop. Safer version: paraphrase the idea, attribute by name ("Harari's argument, roughly, is that money is the widest trust system humans ever built") and never quote more than a single short sentence, and only occasionally, never as the recurring format.

3. "For readers who loved Sapiens" in marketing copy → YES in book descriptions and ad copy, NEVER in KDP keywords. This is standard comp-title marketing language and is defensible as nominative fair use (US) / outside the scope of the EU comparative-advertising rules (not a feature comparison) as long as we (a) don't use Harari's name/image/logo beyond the bare words, (b) never imply he endorses or is affiliated with us, and (c) never put "Sapiens" or "Harari" in the Amazon keyword fields — that one is an explicit, enforced KDP rule and not a grey area.

1. Style and method: where the line actually is

US: Baker v. Selden and the idea/expression dichotomy

US copyright protects expression, not the underlying idea, system, or method — this is the direct holding of Baker v. Selden, 101 U.S. 99 (1879): a copyright on a book describing a bookkeeping system does not give the author exclusive rights over the bookkeeping system itself, only over his specific text and illustrations describing it. (Wikipedia summary; Berkeley Law case history)

Baker v. Selden was codified into statute as 17 U.S.C. § 102(b): "In no case does copyright protection for an original work of authorship extend to any idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation, concept, principle, or discovery, regardless of the form in which it is described, explained, illustrated, or embodied in such work." (full text via FindLaw)

Applied to Harari: the four techniques in docs/notes/harari-method.md (the pendulum, the reframe, thesis-first chapters, sentence-level compression) are each a method of operation — a repeatable structural technique for organizing non-fiction prose. That is exactly the category § 102(b) excludes. A rival bookkeeping form was fine after Baker v. Selden; a rival book using the same essay shape is fine after § 102(b).

Where infringement analysis would actually bite: substantial similarity of protected expression — meaning near-identical sentences, near-identical paragraph structure copying his specific illustrative examples and phrasing (the Peugeot passage word-for-word, say), not the shape of the argument. Courts run a "filtration" step that strips out unprotectable ideas/methods before comparing what's left. (overview of substantial similarity and filtration; Duke Law casebook, ch. 12)

Practical rule: write our own sentences, our own examples, our own dated anecdotes. Never translate/paraphrase a specific Harari passage closely enough that laying the two side by side shows matching sentence-by-sentence structure with substituted nouns. That is the "near-verbatim paraphrase" trap, and it is the one thing in this whole note that could actually lose a case.

EU / Italy: same distinction, different route to it

EU copyright law draws the same idea/expression line, though via case law rather than a single codified subsection. The CJEU's Infopaq decision (C-5/08) established that copyright protection attaches only to elements that are the "author's own intellectual creation" — a standard the CJEU has since used repeatedly to exclude generic ideas, facts, and functional/methodological elements from protection. (EUR-Lex, Infopaq Advocate General opinion)

Italian copyright law (Legge 22 aprile 1941, n. 633) protects "opere dell'ingegno di carattere creativo" (creative intellectual works) in their concrete form of expression; it does not extend to the underlying method, system, or idea, consistent with the Berne Convention's own idea/expression split (Berne Art. 9(2)) which both the US and Italy are bound by as treaty members. (WIPO Lex, full text of Legge 633/1941)

Bottom line for part 1: same answer, both sides of the Atlantic. Style, structure, and rhetorical method are free to use. Sentence-level and example-level copying is not.

2. Verbatim Sapiens quotes as an Instagram marketing hook

US fair use — the four factors, applied honestly

The four factors (17 U.S.C. § 107): (1) purpose and character of the use, including commercial vs. nonprofit; (2) nature of the copyrighted work; (3) amount and substantiality used; (4) effect on the market for the original. (Stanford Copyright & Fair Use overview; Authors Guild fair-use guide for authors)

Applied to "quote card that hooks into our own book, posted repeatedly on a commercial Instagram account":

  • Factor 1 (purpose): Weighs against us. This is advertising — using someone else's words to sell a competing/adjacent commercial product — which is the paradigm "unfair" commercial use the case law and library guidance both flag. (Stanford: commercial uses considered unfair unless transformative) A single one-off quote used for genuine commentary/criticism would weigh differently than a recurring "quote → CTA to buy our book" ad format.
  • Factor 2 (nature of work): Neutral-to-against us — Sapiens is creative, expressive nonfiction, which gets more protection than a purely factual work.
  • Factor 3 (amount): In our favor if the quote is genuinely short (one sentence, not a paragraph) — "two hundred words from a five-hundred-page book" is commonly cited as fair-use territory, unless what's taken is the "heart" of the work (a famous, quotable line — which is exactly the kind of line we'd want to use as a hook). (Nolo, fair use rule)
  • Factor 4 (market effect): Probably not strongly against us — we're not substituting for Sapiens, we're using it as a springboard to our own book. But courts have also recognized a licensing market for exactly this kind of marketing quote (see permissions fees below), and undercutting that market counts against fair use too.

Net: no single short quote automatically loses, but a recurring ad format built on his verbatim words is the weakest fair-use fact pattern of the four — commercial, repeated, not transformative commentary. This is a real risk, not a formality.

EU / Italy — the quotation right is narrower than US fair use, and it doesn't fit our use case well

EU InfoSoc Directive Art. 5(3)(d) lets member states permit quotation "for purposes such as criticism or review," of a work "already lawfully made available to the public," with the source and author's name indicated, "in accordance with fair practice, and to the extent required by the specific purpose." (Two Birds LLP summary of Art. 5(3)(d))

Italy implements this as Art. 70, Legge 633/1941: free use of quotations is permitted "for the purposes of criticism or discussion" (critica o discussione), within the limits justified by that purpose, and — the important clause — provided they do not compete with the economic exploitation of the work; uses for teaching/research must additionally be non-commercial. Attribution (title, author, publisher, translator) is mandatory whenever quoting. (WIPO Lex, Legge 633/1941 full text; commentary on the Italian quotation exception, ipkitten)

This is the key gap: the Italian/EU exception is built for criticism, review, teaching, or discussion — not for marketing. A pure hook-into-a-sales-pitch use is not "criticism or discussion" in the ordinary sense, and it is being used in the course of our own commercial exploitation, which is the exact thing the "do not compete with the economic exploitation of the work" clause is guarding against (arguably not directly competing, since we're not selling Sapiens itself — but this hasn't been tested for our exact use case and Italian courts read the list of qualifying purposes narrowly, per the Italian Supreme Court commentary above).

Practical fix that works under both regimes: frame the card as commentary, not just a hook — quote one line, then respond to it ("Harari says X. Here's what that leaves out —") rather than quote-then-CTA. That's closer to criticism/discussion under Italian law and closer to transformative commentary under US fair use. A bare quote-then-buy-link is the version most exposed on both sides.

What Sapiens' publisher's permissions department would actually require

General trade-publishing practice (not Sapiens-specific — no public, verifiable fee schedule for Sapiens/Vintage found; "not found" for a Sapiens-specific number): permissions departments typically require a formal written request describing the excerpt, the context, and the print run/reach, and charge based on length, prominence (epigraph vs. buried quote), and the requesting book's projected sales. Reported industry ranges: $0–250 for a short prose excerpt (roughly 500–1,000 words) in modest contexts, rising toward $1,000–3,000 in total permissions costs for books that quote regularly and at length; some publishers use a formula tying the fee to (excerpt length ÷ total book length) × royalty rate × print run for substantial extracts. (Publication Coach, on epigraph permissions; Jane Friedman, fair use & permissions guide with sample letter)

For a single-sentence quote used repeatedly across many Instagram cards (effectively an ongoing ad campaign, not a one-off epigraph), a permissions department would likely either quote a real fee or decline outright — publishers are typically more cautious about commercial/advertising use of an author's words than about one-time editorial quotation. We have not contacted Penguin Random House (Sapiens' publisher) to confirm — "not found," flagging as an open action if we ever want to run this format regularly and want it airtight.

Instagram's own terms

Instagram's Terms require that content posted doesn't infringe others' IP, and that users only post what they made or have the right to share. (Instagram/Meta Help Center, Copyright) Meta enforces this via takedown on a copyright complaint from the rights holder, not via pre-screening — so the practical risk isn't Instagram blocking us preemptively, it's a takedown notice or strike if Penguin Random House (or Harari's agent) ever notices and objects. Given our account is a marketing asset for a compounding email/audience strategy, a strike is a real cost even short of a lawsuit.

Practical rule for part 2: treat a bare, repeated, verbatim Sapiens quote as the risky version. Prefer paraphrase-with-attribution as the default marketing hook; reserve a genuine short verbatim quote for occasional use where we add real commentary, not just a CTA.

3. "For readers who loved Sapiens" and similar comp-marketing lines

US trademark: nominative fair use

The controlling test comes from New Kids on the Block v. News America Publishing, Inc., 971 F.2d 302 (9th Cir. 1992): using someone else's mark is non-infringing nominative fair use if (1) the product/service isn't readily identifiable without using the mark, (2) only as much of the mark is used as is reasonably necessary to identify it, and (3) nothing is done that would, together with the mark, suggest sponsorship or endorsement by the mark holder. (Ninth Circuit Model Jury Instructions, nominative fair use; case background, Fashion Law)

"For readers who loved Sapiens" satisfies this cleanly: we need the title to communicate the comp (prong 1), we use only the bare title text, no logo/cover art (prong 2), and the phrasing doesn't claim Harari's endorsement — as long as we don't add anything like "endorsed by" or "as recommended by the author of Sapiens," which would break prong 3.

EU: comparative advertising rules apply, but this phrase probably isn't "comparative advertising" in the strict sense

Directive 2006/114/EC governs comparative advertising: it's lawful only if cumulative conditions are met — not misleading, compares goods/services meeting the same need, objectively compares material/relevant/verifiable/representative features, doesn't discredit or denigrate the competitor's marks, doesn't take unfair advantage of the competitor's reputation, doesn't create confusion, and doesn't present goods as imitations. (EUR-Lex, Directive 2006/114/EC full text)

"For readers who loved Sapiens" is a target-audience descriptor, not a claim comparing verifiable product features (price, quality, specs) — so it likely sits outside the core of what the Directive is built to regulate, and closer to ordinary descriptive/nominative use of a book title to signal genre and audience. The real EU risk isn't the comparative-advertising rules, it's the "unfair advantage of the reputation of a trademark" condition, which functions similarly to the US endorsement concern: don't imply Harari or his publisher backs us, and don't denigrate Sapiens while doing it ("finally, a Sapiens without the mistakes" would be the kind of line that crosses into risk).

Amazon KDP's own rules — this is the one with a hard line

KDP Metadata Guidelines explicitly prohibit "unauthorized reference to other titles or authors" in the Title field, and explicitly list "other authors" and "books by other authors" as prohibited Keywords — described as a serious offense that risks the listing or account. (KDP Metadata Guidelines)

The guidelines do not contain an equivalent explicit prohibition on mentioning other authors/titles in the book description field — indie-author community discussion confirms this is commonly done ("if you loved [book], you'll love this") and is understood as permitted in description copy, just not in keywords/title. (kboards discussion thread, confirming description vs. keyword distinction)

Practical rule for part 3: "for readers who loved Sapiens" is fine in the book description, ad copy, and Instagram captions. Never put "Sapiens" or "Harari" in the 7 KDP keyword slots or in the title/subtitle field — that's a guideline violation with real enforcement, not a judgment call.

What this means operationally, in one place

  • Style/method: copy the technique, never the sentence. Green light, no caveats beyond ordinary "write your own prose."
  • Quote cards: default to paraphrase + attribution. If using a real verbatim quote, keep it to one short sentence, use it occasionally not as the house format, and pair it with actual commentary rather than a bare CTA.
  • "For readers who loved Sapiens": use freely in descriptions/captions/ads. Never in KDP keywords or title. Never imply endorsement. Never disparage Harari or Sapiens while making the comparison.
