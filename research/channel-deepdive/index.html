The day-one operating pattern

Matt holds every account (Instagram, TikTok, YouTube) and posts/uploads everything himself — the agent has no account access, by standing constraint. The agent's job is to hand Matt a ready-to-post batch each week with as little QC friction as possible.

Weekly output, all three platforms:

Platform Cadence Format Agent produces Matt does
Instagram 5 posts/week (~1/weekday) Single-image "text-card" carousels/photos, not Reels, to start 5 draft image-card posts (fact/quote/story text + sourced or generated visual, one consistent template/font/color system), 2-3 alternate hook lines per post, 1 caption per post, a running swipe file of new hooks spotted from winning accounts Final look-check, posts each of the 5 himself, replies to comments/DMs, watches bio-link click-through weekly
TikTok 5-7 posts/week (daily) Single POV/fact hook, 15-30s ("POV: you wake up as [X] in [year] during [event]," or one startling fact tied to the book's topic) Hook/script (one line), shot list or AI-image prompts, caption + hashtags, 5-7 ready-to-post video files (AI voiceover or on-screen text, generated visuals or licensed stock/maps) Uploads and publishes, replies to comments, spot-checks each batch for historical accuracy and tone before it goes out
YouTube Shorts 2-4 Shorts/week (slower than TikTok, on purpose) Slightly more narrative per Short than TikTok — "Weird History Facts," one-eccentric-figure mini-series, or a compressed book-argument fragment — still opens on a hook line, 30-60s Script + hook line, animation/illustration prompts or direction, one consistent narrator voice reused across every video (a brand asset, not a per-video AI voice swap) Uploads, writes titles/thumbnails (YouTube rewards curation more than TikTok does), replies, same accuracy QC pass, and owns setting up any Patreon/description CTA once eligible

Shared pipeline, split execution: the research script (topic → key facts → candidate hooks) and core visual assets (illustrations, maps, AI images, a book-topic style guide) are built once and reused across all three platforms — real production leverage. But the finished units must not be identical files reposted three ways:

  1. Length/density differs — Instagram's text-card is static and self-contained; TikTok is a single-fact/single-scene 15-30s hook; YouTube Shorts carries slightly more narrative at 30-60s.
  2. Voice risk is platform-specific — TikTok's winning accounts openly use AI POV narration and thrive on it; YouTube is actively removing templated AI content at scale (see below), so its Shorts should use one deliberate, consistent narrator voice, not a disposable same-day reuse of the TikTok audio track.
  3. Cadence differs — TikTok rewards near-daily posting; the surviving YouTube Shorts channels are older, slower-cadence, identity-first channels. Cross-posting TikTok's daily hook straight to Shorts would push YouTube toward the templated-volume pattern that got purged in January 2026.

CTA / off-platform routing, day one: copy Instagram's cleanest funnel — one direct link in bio to an email-capture page (Substack/landing page pattern, see @geopolitical.guy below), no linktree layer at first. TikTok has no confirmed off-platform route among any winner researched — treat it as top-of-funnel awareness only, not a direct-to-email channel, until evidence says otherwise. YouTube's confirmed monetization routes are ad revenue (YPP, presumed, not directly confirmed for any single account) and Patreon (confirmed for Fascinating Horror) — add these only once a channel is eligible, not week one.

Not in week one (any platform): Instagram Reels/voiceover, a linktree with multiple destinations, a Premium/paid tier, DM-keyword automation, YouTube Patreon/description CTA setup. None of the failed/weak-funnel accounts below suffered from lacking these on day one — add them once the base cadence is proven to convert.

What cuts against our plan

(a) Winning TikTok accounts run short POV/fact hooks, not long-arc explainer narrative — and our positioning is long-arc explainer. Every winning TikTok account found in this niche runs a short, single-fact or single-scene POV hook ("POV: you wake up in [year] during [event]") — not a sweeping "how this shaped the world" argument. Honest reconciliation: on TikTok, the content unit has to be a fact/hook, not a condensed book chapter. The book's actual argument gets built up across many small hooks over time, not delivered whole in one video. This is a deliberate downgrade from the book's real argument, in service of the format that demonstrably works on this specific platform — it does not mean the positioning is wrong, it means TikTok is not the platform where the full argument gets made.

(b) YouTube purged mass-produced, templated "AI slop" channels in January 2026, and the survivors look hand-made. Verified via direct WebFetch (https://mediacopilot.ai/youtube-purging-ai-slop-channels-creators-ai-tools/, read 2026-08-28): at least 16 channels, tens of millions of subscribers combined, terminated or stripped of monetization for mass-produced/templated, near-identical uploads with "no human creative input." This is not a blanket ban on AI or faceless content — AI-labelled videos with real creative input keep monetization and recommendation eligibility. Every YouTube Shorts winner profiled below has either a distinctive, consistent human/human-sounding narrator tied to the brand, or a long operating history predating the 2025-26 AI-content boom, or both. What this means for an agent-run operation: use one consistent synthetic narrator voice, treated as a real brand asset, across every YouTube Short — never a same-day, per-video AI-voice swap or a disposable "generate 50 near-identical videos" pipeline. That pattern is a real bans/demonetization risk on YouTube specifically, even though the equivalent approach is currently thriving on TikTok.

Failure cases

@sicilyancienthistory / @sicilysancienthistory — deleted (Instagram)

Both https://www.instagram.com/sicilyancienthistory/ and https://www.instagram.com/sicilysancienthistory/ return Instagram's "Profile isn't available" error page (checked live, 2026-08-28). Background (vendor case-study blog, not independently verifiable now the account is gone): OSSA.AI's own write-up claims the account used AI-generated video to grow "0 to nearly 33,100 followers in 6 weeks," one video hitting 1.5M views (https://blog.ossa.ai/sicilyancienthistory-makes-instagram-history-with-ossa-ai/, read 2026-08-28, treat as a vendor's self-reported claim, not verified). What looks different: a rapid AI-content spike followed by total removal is consistent with abandonment, an undisclosed policy ban, or a handle change — Instagram's error page doesn't say which. Lesson: fast AI-driven growth with no real funnel/brand behind it is fragile — followers and views but no email list, product, or platform diversification to survive the account itself disappearing.

@historydefined — main feed stalled despite 313K followers and a real funnel (Instagram)

See full entry above. The more useful failure case for us specifically: it has everything the brief says matters — a linktr.ee funnel, a YouTube presence, 313K followers, five years of runway — and the classic post grid still went quiet, last active post dated February 2026, six months before this read. What looks different from the winners: no evidence of a repeatable weekly production system independent of a person's continued attention. Scale and a funnel are not enough on their own if posting cadence collapses; the winners (howhistorylooks, ancient_origins, geopolitical.guy) are all still posting multiple times a day as of this week.

Explicitly not a blanket ban on AI or faceless content — targets mass-produced, templated, near-identical uploads with "no human creative input"; AI-labelled videos with real creative input keep monetization and recommendation eligibility. - None of these purged examples are history-niche; presented as a verified general platform-risk finding, not a history-specific failure case.

Directional-only signal (not evidence): one anonymous BlackHatWorld forum post (search-snippet, not independently opened) claims the exact same Short with an AI voice got "almost no views" on YouTube but "normal reach" with music instead of AI voice, while the AI-voice version "performs great on TikTok and Facebook" — suggesting YouTube's Shorts algorithm may specifically suppress detectable AI narration more than TikTok's does. Flagged as a signal to watch, not a fact.

What's different about the YouTube survivors: all four Shorts winners have (a) a distinctive, consistent human or human-sounding narrator tied to the brand, or (b) a long operating history predating the 2025-26 AI-content boom (Simple History since 2013, Knowledgia since 2017, Fascinating Horror since 2019), or both.
