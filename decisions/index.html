2026-08-28 — Positioning: the reader who bounced off history

Decision. We position as: short history books that explain how one thing built the world we live in now — no dates to memorise, finished in an evening. Target reader is the tech/business-minded 25-45 year old who found history boring at school and now cannot follow the news because nobody explained the machinery. Full doc: docs/positioning.md.

Why. Reader demand is evidenced and specific — 17 verbatim Reddit threads asking for "a book like Sapiens," with a consistent complaint pattern (too dense, too fast, no chance to absorb, fear of boredom). That complaint IS the positioning. We aim at the complaint, not at the genre.

Rejected: another big-picture history of everything. The format is crowded and the top is closed — Amazon's World History top 20 is 100% traditionally published, zero indie. A solo imprint cannot win on that axis. Our edge has to be topic and hook.

Rejected: the hardcore history-fan audience. Provably huge and provably pays (Fall of Civilizations: 100M+ downloads, 3,920 paying Patreon members; The Rest Is History: 45,000 paying subscribers). But it buys depth and a named human voice, needs years to build, and will punish exactly the detail we cut on purpose.

Rejected as the opening move: "history as a curriculum you finish." The census confirmed this gap is real and empty for adults. But it is the heaviest possible first product, there is no comparable to price against, and building it first breaks the brief's own distribution-before-production rule. Kept as the destination once a backlist exists, not the starting line.

Sub-decision: the faceless rule survives. The census found every scaled history newsletter is a named person, which looked like a direct conflict. Resolved by treating it as a channel constraint, not a business one: faceless newsletters fail because a newsletter is a relationship with a person, but faceless short video wins at multi-million scale in this exact niche. So the imprint stays faceless and builds on short video; the email list is the destination, not the brand. No pen-name persona, no invented author.

Sub-decision: we signal the Harari promise using the readers' own words, never his name. "Big picture, not a list of events." "Explains why it mattered, not when it happened." "You'll actually finish it." Honest, because it describes our book, and it lands with the person searching for that feeling.

Known gap. Google Trends and all keyword-volume tools were blocked this round. There is no search-volume validation for any specific topic, The Dollar Empire included. Positioning does not depend on it; topic choice does. Must be closed before book one is locked.

Static cards are also the cheapest unit an agent can produce (no video render, no voice, no footage), the faceless proof is strongest here (live-fetched comps 74.8K to 6.3M), and Matt already holds the account.

Rejected: Reddit, despite the highest reach score of any channel — bot posting is banned outright, so it only works with Matt posting and replying, and Matt is the constraint.

Rejected: paid newsletter first (slowest path to a first sale, needs a list we don't have) and Amazon internal search first (highest intent, but only activates once a book exists — sequenced last).

Followers grow but emails don't → funnel is broken, fix the link, keep the content. Neither grows → switch to the parked TikTok channel on its POV/fact-hook format.

Known weakness. All three conversion rates are borrowed from outside our niche — no first-party figure for an account like ours exists. The funnel is a plan for what to measure, not a forecast. The first 60 days replace all three numbers.

2026-08-28 — Offer: a $4.99 short ebook, free first three chapters

Decision. Two rungs only. Free: The Dollar, in three chapters — the real opening third of book one, EPUB+PDF, delivered by the email capture page. Paid: The Dollar Empire, ~20,000 words, $4.99 ebook / $12.99 paperback, Amazon KDP first and direct (Payhip/Gumroad) second. Full doc: docs/offer.md.

Series, shipped one at a time. Books 2 and 3 named before book 1 ships (World Wars → America; British Empire) so the imprint reads as a series from day one — but we write exactly one. The graveyard says a book with no backlist has nothing to compound, and every dead operator was a one-shot.

Short (~20k words) because length IS the reader complaint we're aiming at ("too dense, too fast, no chance to absorb"), and because it lets book two exist this year.

Amazon first because KDP is the only model where Amazon supplies the stranger — a cold buyer finds and buys with zero audience-building. Every other model requires us to produce the visitor first. Direct sale runs alongside (nets ~$4.40 vs Amazon's ~$3.44 on the same $4.99): Amazon gets strangers, our list gets the direct link.

$4.99, not $2.99 or $9.99. Inside KDP's 70% band. Impulse-buyable from a phone right after an Instagram post — the exact buying moment we're engineering. $2.99 reads disposable; $9.99 for 20k words from an unknown imprint earns bad reviews, and reviews are the one asset an indie title competes on.

Free thing is three real chapters, not a lead-magnet PDF. The best predictor of buying our book is having liked reading our book.

Rejected: the curriculum site (the real, confirmed gap) — no adult history curriculum exists anywhere to price against (not found, nothing at all), heaviest possible build, breaks distribution-before-production. Arrives at 3-4 books, not now. Rejected: paid newsletter (slowest to first sale, needs a personality we ruled out). Rejected: monthly subscription (a cadence we'd break — the exact shape of the quiet death in the graveyard). Rejected: Patreon (requires a show; we're not making a show).

Amazon tactic that matters: indie titles rank only in narrow Kindle subcategories. A subcategory #1 with ~38 ratings is reachable; the World History top 20 (100% trad-published) is not. Categories and keywords get chosen deliberately.

Honest risk. No search-volume evidence exists for "The Dollar Empire" specifically — all keyword tools were blocked. It was chosen from Matt's list because it fits the promise, not because appetite was measured. The offer structure survives a topic change; the topic is the open question.

House style derived without a copy of Sapiens

Date: 2026-08-30

The Sapiens file is not on this machine and the task was blocking the book. Rather than wait, the method was reconstructed from open sources: Goodreads verbatim quote banks, a full-text mirror, LitCharts chapter guides, the NPR excerpt, and the critical literature (Hallpike, Narayanan, Lent and others). Three parallel researchers covered structure, sentence craft, and the reframe technique.

Decided: docs/house-style.md is canonical now, at v1. Two gaps are logged in docs/notes/harari-method.md to close when the file arrives — a verbatim species-to-single-body passage, and per-chapter word counts.

Also decided — we hedge harder than Harari does. Sapiens is attacked on eight specific evidence charges (uncited sweeping claims, contested theories stated as settled, false binaries, just-so stories). All eight are evidence handling, none touch the rhetorical technique, so the style guide takes the method and bans the sin. The commercial reason: our reader already fears they cannot trust their own understanding of history. Being caught in an error confirms that fear and is fatal for us in a way it is not for a writer with a global platform.

Also decided — 35,000 words, 8 chapters of ~4,000. Sapiens runs ~6,000 words a chapter over 20 chapters, and "too dense, no chance to absorb" is the exact complaint our reader made about it in the demand research.

Rejected: waiting for the file (blocks the book indefinitely, and Matt may never find it). Rejected: buying or downloading a copy (we buy nothing). Rejected: writing a style guide from general "popular history" principles without researching Harari specifically — that would have produced platitudes, and Matt asked what zooming out actually means, which needs the real technique.

2026-08-31 — The Harari line: use the method, never his words

Decided. We write in Harari's method freely, and we never quote him verbatim on an Instagram card. Where his argument is the entry point, we paraphrase it, name him as its author, and answer it in our own words. "For readers who loved Sapiens" is allowed in captions and descriptions, and banned from every KDP metadata field. Research and sources: docs/notes/harari-ip.md. Rules live in docs/house-style.md.

Why. Style, structure and ideas are not copyrightable anywhere we sell; expression is. A repeat commercial campaign built on his verbatim sentences is the weakest fair-use fact pattern in the US and falls outside the EU/Italian quotation right, which exists for criticism, not for marketing. The paraphrase-and-answer format carries no risk and makes better cards — a disagreement hooks harder than a quote.

Rejected — quote cards as the entry format. Matt's own suggestion, and the single highest-reach format on history Instagram. Killed because the risk lands on the one asset we cannot replace: an Amazon listing or an Instagram account, both removable on a complaint with no appeal worth having.

Rejected — licensing quotes from the publisher. Permissions departments quote per-territory fees for marketing use and take weeks. Weeks and money, for a hook we can get free by paraphrasing.

Rejected — never mentioning him at all. Over-cautious. Nominative reference in body copy is legal and is how every comparable indie title finds its reader.

2026-08-31 — The reader's gap is mechanism, not missing history

Decision. Positioning rewritten on Matt's framing. The target reader is no longer "the one who bounced off history" but the one who was taught history and still cannot explain the world. The product sells mechanism — how tools, communication, shared stories and power actually work — not events. New one sentence: "School taught you what happened. These books explain how it works — one force at a time, and you finish each one in an evening."

Consequence, and it is a hard filter. A title must name a force. If a proposed book cannot finish "this is how X let humans do something they could not do before," it is not our book. This constrains catalogue directly.

Second consequence. With mechanism as the subject, the books stop being a shelf of separate topics and become one explanation in parts. The curriculum angle, previously rejected as far-off, moves much closer — it is now the natural shape of the catalogue rather than a later add-on.

Options rejected: - Keep "bounced off history." Rejected: it aims at a smaller group and implies the fix is history-but-nicer, which is the four-hundredth Sapiens clone. - "History for people who forgot it all." Rejected: insulting to a well-educated reader who feels he should already know this, and he is the buyer. - Lead on "no dates." Rejected as the lead, kept as promise 2. "No dates" says what we removed. It does not say what the reader gets.

Four forces × four books also fills each lever without padding. Sixteen is roughly a year at a book every three weeks.

Rejected: an open-ended list. "Write books until we stop" gives us no bundle, no finish line and no way to say the reader is done. The whole pitch is you will finish it — that has to be true of the shelf as well as the book.

Rejected: 8 books. Too thin to justify a $69 bundle, and it forces us to cut either stories or power, both of which the reader needs to read the news.

Rejected: 25+ (the full curriculum). Unwritable this year, and it turns the imprint back into the endless-feed model that killed every operator in the graveyard.

Rejected: publishing in reading order. Book one would be The Surplus (farming) — logically first, commercially the weakest hook on Instagram. Split the two orders instead; nobody buying book one knows or cares that it is number nine.

Live risk. Search-volume tools were all blocked, so the publishing order is judgement. The presale item tests it: all sixteen go up as coming-soon with a per-title notify button, and clicks reorder the list.

2026-08-31 — The bundle is the hero; Amazon is discovery, not the shop

Decision. $9 a single ebook, $69 for the Essential Sixteen against a $144 shelf price, bundle sold direct only and pushed everywhere. Amazon carries the single titles for cold discovery and reviews. Written up in docs/offer.md, with the mechanics sourced in docs/notes/amazon-and-formats.md.

Rejected: KDP Select / Kindle Unlimited. Select demands 90-day exclusivity — the same ebook may not be sold anywhere else, including our own site, beyond a 10% excerpt. That deletes the bundle, the free three chapters and the direct list at once. KENP page-reads (~$0.004–$0.005/page) do not buy that back.

Rejected: Amazon as the shop we push. On a $9 ebook Amazon nets us ~$6.24 (70% minus ~$0.06 delivery); our own store nets ~$8.55. Amazon is worth having for the strangers it brings, not the margin, and it cannot sell the bundle at all.

Rejected: paperback, for now. POD costs nothing to start but needs a trim-size interior PDF and a spine-width-dependent cover spread per title — sixteen times, against Matt's 3–5 hours a week. Covers are type-only, and the field colour encodes the part

Rejected — bespoke illustration or a cutaway diagram per title. It is the obvious "technical drawing" move and it fails three ways: sixteen hand-made drawings is weeks we do not have, generated art reads as AI at thumbnail size, and a diagram that does not point at a real part of the argument is ornament — banned by rule 1 of the design system.

Rejected — a warmer or per-book accent colour to tell the sixteen apart. Two field colours plus rationed red is the whole palette. Sixteen colours is a rainbow shelf and kills the "one imprint" read, which is the only reason the shelf sells before the books exist.

Rejected — hiding covers until a book is written. Matt's point stands: a shop with no covers looks broken. Every cover carries COMING SOON in the bottom-right state field, so it shows the shelf without claiming a book exists.

The shelf is a ballot: a free waitlist per title, not a presale

Date: 2026-08-31 · Item: presale

The sixteen-cover shelf at /covers/ is now the interest capture. Every cover is a checkbox. The reader taps the books they want, leaves one email once, and the picks travel with the address. We publish in the order the clicks tell us to, instead of the order we guessed.

Rejected — a real presale, money for an unwritten book. Two hard blocks, both already logged in offer.md: EU distance-selling law gives a 14-day refund right that an undelivered book cannot survive cleanly, and Amazon bans an account from preorders for a year if it misses a preorder date. A presale also measures the wrong thing at the wrong time — it asks for $9 from a stranger who has read nothing of ours.

Rejected — sixteen separate "notify me" forms, one per cover. Sixteen forms is sixteen decisions and sixteen chances to leave. One ballot plus one email box is one decision. It also lets a reader vote for three books, which is the reading we actually want: not "who wants The Chokepoint" but "what does a person who likes us want next".

Rejected — putting the ballot on the landing page. The landing page has one action and keeps it. The shelf is a second surface with a second job, and it is the page where sixteen covers already justify themselves.

Rejected — a public vote counter on the page. Honest, and a trap: an early leader snowballs, and a low number tells every visitor that nobody is here. We read the counts privately.

News-led carousels become 2 of the 5 weekly posts, not all 5

Date: 2026-08-31 · Item: news-hook

Matt's worked example — "Hormuz is in the news and you have no idea what it means" — is the strongest hook we have written, so the question was whether the whole Instagram account should lead from today's news instead of the essentials. The answer is a ratio, not a switch: two news-led carousels a week, three evergreen. Format spec and the three rules are in docs/distribution.md; the proof set is data/news/, three Hormuz cards landing on The Barrel (Book 03), live at /news/.

News-led wins the top of the funnel for three reasons. Instagram's recommendation engine rides existing attention, and a story already in the feed is attention we do not have to manufacture. It demonstrates the promise instead of asserting it — card 01 of the evergreen set says "you can follow the news, you just can't follow why," and a news carousel proves that claim every week instead of repeating it. And it is an endless supply of hooks we do not have to invent.

Rejected — going all-news. Three costs, and together they are decisive. News cards are perishable: they die in a week, while the evergreen set is what a new follower scrolls back through and what can be reposted forever. News drags us toward commentary, which positioning.md refuses — we sell mechanism, not opinion. And most news does not map cleanly to one of the sixteen forces; an all-news schedule forces the mapping and produces cards that sell nothing.

Rejected — staying all-evergreen. It is what we already built, and it asks a stranger to care about the Bretton Woods system before they have any reason to. The news is the reason.

The rule that makes it safe: we never say what should happen. No sides, no prediction, no blame — only how the machine got built. Every geopolitics account on Instagram takes a position. Not taking one is the difference, and it is also what keeps the comments from becoming a fight we have no author persona to fight.

Cost of being wrong: low, and measurable in four weeks. The ratio is one number. If the news carousels out-reach the evergreen ones by a wide margin we move to 3/2 or 4/1; if they underperform we drop to one a week. The evergreen set stays either way.

2026-08-31 — The grid anchors to the text, and paper surfaces lose the horizontal lines

Decision. On flat-field surfaces (Instagram card, book cover) the full grid stays and the padding becomes a whole number of modules: card 80/80/70 at 40px, cover 100/100/110 at 50px.

Why. Matt: the look is right but text over the background feels messy. Cause was mechanical — every surface drew the grid from its own edge while content sat at 72px / 108px / 24px, none of them multiples of the module, and the landing column is centred so the offset moved with the browser width. Nothing ever aligned.

Rejected. - A true baseline grid. 19px serif at 1.6 leading is ~30px, fits neither a 20 nor a 40 rhythm; forcing it makes the body copy cramped or airy and still breaks when a heading rewraps. - Hand-snapping each hairline. A rule deep in the page inherits the cumulative height of everything above it — one edited sentence breaks it. - Keeping the viewport-anchored grid with nicer padding numbers. Impossible for a centred column: the offset changes with every viewport width.

2026-08-31 — No Harari quote account. We steal the mechanic, not the source.

Decision. We do not run a Navalism-style account reposting Harari's words. We do take the mechanic behind it — borrowed authority as the door — and we already use it: the news-led carousel (Hormuz). The one new variant worth testing is a primary-source quote card: verbatim lines from the people who built the force we explain (Nixon 1971 on the gold window, Smith, Keynes, a treaty, a memo), all old enough to be free to use, each one paired with our explanation of what it set in motion. Queued as todo (primary-source-quotes), not started.

Why. Three separate blocks against the Harari version. 1. Legal. docs/notes/harari-ip.md already rules on this exact fact pattern: repeated verbatim quoting, commercial, not criticism, as the recurring format is the use that fails fair use in both the US and the EU. Doing it as the format is the worst version. Naval tolerates Navalism because it grows him; Harari's publisher has no such reason to tolerate a rival imprint doing it. 2. Positioning. We sell mechanism — how a thing works. A quote account sells aphorism. The Sapiens lines that travel are the deepity ones, and they explain nothing. We would be training an audience on the opposite of our product. 3. Conversion. A quote account's followers are loyal to the quoted person, not to the account. The day we sell a book the audience asks "who are you?" — and the honest answer is "the people who reposted the man you actually came for."

Rejected. - Run it as a separate account and cross-post. Doubles the work on our one channel, and the crossover is exactly where the implied-endorsement risk lives. - Occasional Harari quotes inside our own cards. Already allowed by harari-ip.md (single short sentence, attributed, rare) — kept as an option, but it is not a format and does not answer Matt's question.

Cost of being wrong: low. The primary-source card is one carousel to test.

2026-09-01 — The shelf is a coming-soon page, not a ballot

Decision. /covers/ drops the checkboxes and the "we write in the order you choose" promise. It becomes sixteen covers grouped by part, each with its deck, and one email box: want to know when they are ready? Emails go to Matt's inbox through FormSubmit.co — no database, no list platform, no account for the agent to create.

Why. Matt's call, and it is right. A public vote tells the reader we have no editorial plan; an imprint that asks its audience what to publish reads as amateur. The publishing order is our job. Matt also said explicitly: do the product, the design and the distribution first, and the email machinery later.

Options rejected. - Keep the ballot, blocked on a Brevo form URL. The design we shipped 2026-08-31. Truly zero-dependency, and we can never read a single address. - Netlify Forms. Free and elegant, but the site is on GitHub Pages; moving hosts to collect emails is the tail wagging the dog.

Known cost. FormSubmit needs one activation click from Matt the first time someone submits, and until then submissions vanish. The page never shows the reader a failure — it posts and thanks them either way — so the cost is silent lost addresses in the first hours, not a broken page. Matt's address is assembled in JS from parts rather than sitting in the HTML source, which stops naive scrapers and nothing more.
