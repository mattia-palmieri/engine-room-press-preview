Fig. 01 — The problem

School taught you history as a list of things that happened in an order. So you decided history was boring, and you were right — that version is.

Then you got older, started reading the news properly, and hit a wall. Why does the dollar decide what a country in another hemisphere can afford to eat? Why do those borders sit exactly there? Nobody ever explained the machinery. They just gave you the dates.

Fig. 02 — The three promises

  • 01 One idea per book.

    Not all of history at once. One thing, explained until you can actually use it.

  • 02 No dates to memorise.

    If a name or a year isn't doing real work, it's cut.

  • 03 You'll finish it.

    Short on purpose — an evening, not a season. The point is to understand something, not to own a thick book.

Fig. 05 — Why this exists

“I want something that explains why things mattered, not when they happened. Every book I get recommended is either 900 pages or a list of facts.” The request, in one form or another, over and over.

We went looking for the book that answers that, and kept not finding it. So we're making it.

Fig. 06 — What we won't do

  • No trivia. "Ten facts you won't believe" is not understanding.
  • No completeness. We leave things out on purpose, and we'll say which.
  • No lectures for people who already know. If a specialist is annoyed by what we cut, we cut correctly.
