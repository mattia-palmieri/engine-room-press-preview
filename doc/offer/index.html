Two rungs. Nothing else. No subscription, no course, no membership, no Patreon.

The decisions, one by one

A series, not one book — but shipped one at a time. The graveyard is unambiguous: a single book with no backlist has nothing to compound, and every dead operator was a one-shot. Books two and three are named before book one ships (the World Wars and how they handed the century to America, the British Empire), so the imprint reads as a series from the first day. But we write and ship exactly one.

Short: ~20,000 words. An evening's read. This is the positioning made physical. The reader complaint we are aiming at is too dense, too fast, no chance to absorb, bored out of my mind — length is the complaint. It also means book two can exist this year, which the graveyard says matters more than book one being perfect.

Amazon first, direct second. KDP is the only model in the revenue research where Amazon supplies the stranger: a cold buyer can find and buy with zero audience-building on our side. Everything else — direct sale, newsletter, course — requires us to produce the visitor first. Days-to-first-paying-stranger is the metric, and KDP wins it mechanically, not just anecdotally. Direct sale (Payhip, 5% fee, or Gumroad) goes up alongside because it nets ~$4.40 on the same $4.99 versus Amazon's ~$3.44, and because buyers who come through our own list should be ours. Amazon gets the strangers; the list gets the direct link.

Price: $4.99. Inside KDP's 70% royalty band ($2.99–$12.99), which nets ~$3.44 an ebook. Cheap enough to buy on impulse from a phone after one Instagram post, which is exactly the buying moment we are engineering. Not $2.99 — that prices it as disposable, and we need the paperback at $12.99 to look like a real book beside it. Not $9.99 — a 20,000-word book from an unknown imprint at $9.99 is a bad deal and readers will say so in reviews, and reviews are the one asset an indie title actually competes on.

Paperback at $12.99 from day one. Same listing, no extra work beyond formatting, and print-on-demand costs nothing until someone buys. It exists mostly to make the ebook look correctly priced.

The free thing is three real chapters, not a bonus PDF. Not a "guide," not a checklist, not a listicle. The single best predictor of whether someone buys our book is whether they liked reading our book. Give them a third of it. It ends on the question the rest answers.

What is deliberately NOT in the ladder

The curriculum site. The census found "history as a curriculum you finish" genuinely does not exist for adults, and it is the most interesting gap we have. It stays out of the ladder for now: revenue-models found no adult history curriculum anywhere to price against (not found, not a low number — nothing at all), it is the heaviest possible build, and the brief's own rule is distribution before production. It arrives when there are three or four books to sequence, and not before.

A paid newsletter. Slowest path to a first sale in the entire revenue set, and structurally needs a personality — which we have ruled out.

A subscription "new book each month." A monthly cadence is a promise we would break, and a broken cadence is the exact shape of the quiet death in the graveyard.

Patreon. Requires an ongoing show. We are not making a show.

What has to be true

  1. Book one is genuinely good — Matt is reader one, and if it bores him it ships to nobody.
  2. The free three chapters are the actual opening of the book, not a re-cut. Writing them twice wastes the only real asset.
  3. The email capture page exists before the first Instagram post.
  4. The KDP listing gets its categories and keywords chosen deliberately. The indie titles that rank do so in narrow Kindle subcategories — that is the whole game on Amazon for an unknown imprint. A subcategory #1 with 38 ratings is a real, reachable target; the World History top 20 is not.

The honest risk

We are pricing and formatting against a topic we have not demand-validated. Google Trends and every keyword tool were blocked, so there is no search-volume evidence for "The Dollar Empire" specifically — it was chosen from Matt's own list because it fits the promise, not because we measured appetite for it. The offer structure survives a topic change. The topic itself is the open question.
