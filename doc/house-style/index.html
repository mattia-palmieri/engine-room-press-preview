How Engine Room Press books are written — the four zoom-out techniques, the sentence rules, the evidence discipline, and the checklist every chapter must pass.

House style

Write against this. You do not need to have read Sapiens to use it — the research behind it is in docs/notes/harari-method.md.

The promise this style has to keep

One force per book. Why it mattered, how it changed the world. No dates to memorise. You finish it in an evening. Every rule below exists to deliver one of those four.

Target: ~35,000 words, 8 chapters of ~4,000. Shorter than Sapiens by design — Sapiens is ~6,000 words a chapter over 20 chapters, and "too dense, no chance to absorb" is the exact complaint our reader made about it.

1. The pendulum

Swing between one named human and the whole species. Never rest in the middle.

This is the technique the reader calls "big picture." It is not a wide-angle lens. It is a fast cut between the widest shot and the tightest one.

The move, in three beats:

  1. Anchor. One named person, one dated event, one object you could hold. "In 1896 Armand Peugeot paid a lawyer to perform the procedures."
  2. Name the mechanism. Give it a label. "Lawyers call this a legal fiction."
  3. Jump to n=all. Straight to the species or the century. No "and other firms did likewise." The jump is binary.

Banned: the middle distance. "Several other merchants in the region also adopted the practice" is the sentence that kills the effect. It is the register of a textbook, and the textbook is what our reader bounced off.

The label is the landing. You land a zoom-out by naming the pattern, not by piling on more cases. "The luxury trap." "The imagined order." Coin one term per book, define it the moment you coin it, then reuse it unchanged so it becomes the reader's vocabulary. One coinage per book. Two is clutter.

Zooming out removes people. So zoom back in fast. From altitude, individual humans are invisible. A reader with nobody to stand next to stops reading. Rule of thumb: no more than two paragraphs at species scale before a body appears.

2. The reframe

One per chapter. Eight per book. Each is a reversal, not a fact.

Take a belief so ordinary the reader never noticed it was a claim, and turn its load-bearing word around.

  • we domesticated wheat → it domesticated us
  • science accumulates facts → science began by admitting ignorance
  • history bends toward justice → there is no justice in history

A topic is reframe-able only if all three hold:

  1. The assumption is stated as self-evident, never argued for.
  2. There is a plausible reversed direction the reader has never been offered.
  3. Once said, it feels obvious in hindsight.

Emotional weight is required. Progress, fairness, safety, human specialness. Reversing a piece of trivia gets you nothing — the "oh no" is the product.

State the flip in a short, flat, near-aphoristic sentence. Then illustrate.

The honesty clause is mandatory. A reframe is a lens, not a finding. Say so, once, plainly, in the chapter: "this is one way to read it, and here is who disagrees." One clause. It costs nothing and it is the difference between a good book and a viral thread about our errors. See §5.

3. Chapter shape

Thesis first. It is an essay, never a mystery.

TITLE        a conclusion, not a topic
OPENING      the claim restated at maximum scale, or a direct
             instruction to the reader
BODY         3-5 sections under hook subheads; concrete zoom-in
             evidence that cashes out the title
CLOSE        re-abstract, higher and darker than the opening;
             leave it open
  • Titles are verdicts. "History's Biggest Fraud," not "The Agricultural Revolution." If the title could head a Wikipedia section, rewrite it.
  • Openings are not scenes. Do not write "It was a cold morning in Genoa." Open with a flat claim at scale, or tell the reader what they are about to do: "To understand X we have to get inside the head of Y." The named human arrives one or two paragraphs later, as the payoff.
  • Closes do not recap. End on a generalisation, unsettling, unresolved. The next chapter's first line answers it. Recap paragraphs are a textbook tic and they tell the reader the chapter is over before it is.
  • Subheads are hooks, never labels. "The Luxury Trap," "Signed, Kushim." Never "Economic Consequences." Count tracks breadth, not length: 3 for one sustained argument, up to 6 if the chapter is really several linked essays.
  • Front-load the book's ending on page one. State where this is all going in the introduction, then land on it in the last line. The reader should feel the book close a loop.

4. Sentences

Rhythm. Two to four medium declaratives (15–25 words) do the reasoning. Then one short sentence, under eight words, delivers the verdict. The short one carries no new information — it restates the long one in plainer words. "We did not domesticate wheat. It domesticated us."

Dates and numbers. - Round and flat. "Seventy thousand years ago." Never "circa," never "c. 68,000 BCE," never a decimal point. Roundness reads as confidence. - Anchor relatively wherever you can: "three centuries before Columbus sailed." The reader should never have to hold an absolute year. - The no-memorising rule, hard version: if a year, name or place is not doing argumentative work in the sentence it sits in, cut it. Ask of every proper noun: would the paragraph survive without it? Then it goes. - Never make scale felt with more digits. Make it felt with a body doing a physical thing.

Metaphor is physical and domestic. Plants, animals, weather, metal, money in a pocket, paperwork. Never abstract mapped onto abstract. If the reader's hands have not touched it, find another one.

Questions cluster and open paragraphs. Never close on one. Either a question with a fragment answer, or three questions narrowing the space before the real answer arrives. "Who was responsible? Neither kings, nor priests, nor merchants."

"We" about twice as often as "you." "We" implicates the reader in something done long before they were born, and that is the whole emotional engine. Save "you" for the moments the reader should check the claim against their own life.

Humour is deadpan, in the same flat voice. No exclamation marks. No visible wink. The target is always the gap between what people think they are doing and how arbitrary it is. Gold: "a metal that could not be eaten, drunk or woven." The joke is one word.

Jargon is defused in the sentence that introduces it. Never let a term sit undefined for a paragraph. Best move: introduce it and immediately undercut it.

Plain words. Short, Anglo-Saxon, concrete. If an abstract noun is unavoidable, put a concrete verb or object in the same sentence with it.

5. Evidence discipline — the rules Harari breaks

Our reader bounced off history and feels ignorant. The one thing that would destroy us with them is being caught saying something false: it confirms their fear that they cannot trust their own understanding. Harari survives his critics because he has a global platform. We would not survive one viral thread. Our hedging has to be better than his.

None of this costs us the style. Every technique above is rhetorical structure. The sins below are all evidence handling. Take the method, leave the sin.

Non-negotiable:

  • Never state a contested theory as settled. Where historians actively disagree, say so and name the fight in one sentence. A live argument is more interesting than a closed one — this usually improves the page.
  • Match the hedge to the size of the claim. A species-level, ten-thousand-year claim needs real support or it gets demoted to "one plausible reading."
  • No confident citation-free sweep. Tone is not evidence. This is the single most repeated charge against Sapiens.
  • No false binaries. Happy foragers versus miserable farmers. If the record has three live positions, do not spine a chapter on two.
  • Name your sources in the prose. "The historian X argues" costs four words and buys enormous trust. Never reuse another writer's framework or coinage without naming them.
  • Pick a moral lane. Do not debunk something as invented and then draw outrage from it being real two chapters later.
  • The title may promise energy. It may not promise certainty the chapter does not deliver.

Every book ships with a short "where I might be wrong" note and a real reading list. That is not an apology. It is the trust device, and it is also the thing no competitor bothers with.

Chapter checklist

Before a chapter is done:

  • [ ] The title is a verdict, not a topic.
  • [ ] One reframe, clearly placed, with its honesty clause.
  • [ ] At least three pendulum swings — named human to species and back.
  • [ ] No paragraph sits in the middle distance.
  • [ ] Never more than two paragraphs at species scale without a body.
  • [ ] Every proper noun and year is doing work. The rest are cut.
  • [ ] Scale is carried by an analogy a reader could touch, not by digits.
  • [ ] The close is a re-abstraction, higher and open. It does not recap.
  • [ ] Every contested claim is flagged as contested, in one clause.
  • [ ] Read aloud: the long/short rhythm is audible, and the verdict sentences land.
