We are the imprint for adults who want to understand how the modern world got built, one force per short book, with no dates to memorise — sold from faceless short video, not from a personal brand.

Positioning

The one sentence

Short history books that explain how one thing built the world we live in now — no dates to memorise, and you finish it in an evening.

That is the sentence a stranger has to understand. Everything below is why it is that sentence and not another.

Who it is for

Not "curious adults." Too big to aim at.

The reader who bounced off history and now regrets it. Aged roughly 25-45, tech or business minded, reads non-fiction to understand the world. School taught them dates and they concluded history was boring. Then they got older, started reading the news, and found they could not follow it — money, empires, borders, religion — because nobody ever explained the machinery. They do not want to become a history person. They want to stop feeling ignorant.

This is a real, evidenced segment, not a guess. On Reddit, 17 separate threads ask for "a book like Sapiens," and the complaints about what gets recommended are consistent: too dense, too fast, no chance to absorb, "bored out of my mind." They are not asking for more history. They are asking for history they can actually finish.

Explicitly not for: hardcore history fans (they know the material and will judge us on the detail we deliberately cut), and students or exam-takers (they want exactly the detail we remove). Both rejected in the brief and both confirmed by the research — the Hardcore History / Fall of Civilizations audience is enormous and completely wrong for us.

What we promise

One force per book. Why it mattered, how it changed the world, how it fits the whole picture. Not a survey. Not a timeline. One thing, explained until the reader can use it.

Three promises we can make honestly and repeat everywhere:

  1. One idea per book. You will not be handed all of human history at once.
  2. No dates to memorise. If a name or a year is not doing work, it is cut.
  3. You will finish it. Short by design — an evening, not a season.

How we signal the Harari promise without saying his name

We never say "reads like Harari." We do not need to. The readers asking for him described what they want in their own words, and those words are free to use:

  • "Big picture, not a list of events."
  • "Explains why it mattered, not when it happened."
  • "Makes the world make sense."
  • "I actually finished it."

We borrow the reader's vocabulary, not the author's name. The category signal is carried by the promise triple above, which is a fair description of our own book — so it is honest, and it lands with exactly the person searching for the same feeling.

The faceless problem, and how it is resolved

The census found something that fights the brief: every history newsletter with real scale is a named human. No faceless brand has ever built a large history newsletter. That looked like a direct conflict with the faceless rule.

It is not a conflict. It is a channel constraint that has been mis-attributed to the whole business.

Faceless newsletters fail because a newsletter is a relationship with a person — you subscribe to someone. Faceless short video is a different medium entirely, and it works at multi-million scale in exactly our niche: the channel research found real faceless history and explainer accounts in the millions of followers, with no face and no persona anywhere in them.

So: the imprint is faceless, and we build on short video, not on a personal newsletter. The email list still exists, but it is the destination, not the brand — people join it to get the next book, not to hear from a person. That is a list an imprint can own.

Decision: faceless stays. No pen-name persona, no invented author. We route around the constraint by picking the channel where faceless already wins.

Why us

Nothing about our skills. Two structural reasons only:

  1. Matt is reader number one. He has the exact need and no tolerance for boring. If a book bores him it ships to nobody. That is a real quality gate most indie history publishing does not have.
  2. We can produce at a cadence a person cannot. A one-person imprint that ships a short book on a schedule, plus daily short video, is only possible because agents do the volume. Every dead operator in the graveyard died quietly of burnout. We are not exposed to that the same way.

What we refuse to be

  • Not a personality. No face, no pen name, no "hi guys."
  • Not a trivia channel. No "10 facts you didn't know." That audience does not buy books.
  • Not comprehensive. We will be wrong-by-omission on purpose, and we say so.
  • Not for the expert. If a specialist is annoyed by what we cut, we cut correctly.
  • Not free forever. Ad-funded and grant-funded products in the graveyard died when the funder left. We sell to readers so readers keep us alive.

Three angles named and rejected

1. "The Sapiens shelf" — another big-picture history of everything. Rejected. The census found this format is already crowded and the top of it is closed: Amazon's World History top 20 is 100% traditionally published, zero indie titles. We would be the four-hundredth book competing on the one axis where a solo imprint cannot win. The format is not our edge — the topic and the hook are.

2. "History for hardcore fans" — depth, rigour, the Fall of Civilizations audience. Rejected, and it is the tempting one, because the audience is provably huge and provably pays: Fall of Civilizations has 100M+ downloads and 3,920 paying Patreon members; The Rest Is History has 45,000 paying subscribers. But that audience buys depth and voice, both of which need years and a named human. It is also the audience most likely to catch and punish the detail we deliberately cut. Wrong reader, wrong product, and the brief rejects it outright.

3. "History as a curriculum you finish" — the gap we found. Rejected as the opening angle, kept as the destination. The census confirmed this genuinely does not exist for adults, which is real. But a curriculum is the heaviest possible first product: you cannot sell class one of a course nobody has heard of, revenue-models found no adult history curriculum anywhere to price against, and building it first inverts the brief's own rule of distribution before production. So: books first, and the "read them in order and finish the set" framing arrives as the series grows. The gap is real. It is just not a starting line.

What this positioning commits us to next

  • The first book is one force, not a survey. The Dollar Empire fits the promise exactly — it is the machinery behind news the reader already reads.
  • The channel is faceless short video, feeding an email list owned by the imprint.
  • The first paid thing is a short book, priced so a stranger can buy on impulse.

Those three carry into distribution.md and offer.md.
