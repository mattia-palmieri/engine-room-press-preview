For adults who were taught history and still cannot explain the world — we sell the machinery (tools, money, stories, power), one force per short book, no dates, faceless on Instagram.

Positioning

The one sentence

School taught you what happened. These books explain how it works — one force at a time, and you finish each one in an evening.

That is the sentence a stranger has to understand. Everything below is why it is that sentence and not another.

The correction this version makes

The old positioning aimed at "the reader who bounced off history." That is too small, and it is slightly wrong. It implies the fix is a second attempt at the same subject — history, but nicer.

The real reader may have sat through years of history and still cannot explain the world. He got good marks. He knows there was a Roman Empire, a Reformation, a Cold War. And he still cannot say why the dollar is the world's money, why the Middle East is drawn the way it is, or why a religion can move a hundred million people. Nothing was missing from his education except the only part that mattered.

The gap is not knowledge. It is mechanism. History was delivered as a list of what happened. Nobody delivered how anything works.

That is exactly the trick Harari runs, and it is why people who did fine at school still say Sapiens was the first history that made sense to them. He does not add facts. He explains the machinery underneath them:

  • Tools and technology let humans do things they could not do before. The plough makes surplus, surplus makes cities, cities make kings.
  • Communication multiplies whatever it touches. Writing, print, the wire, the packet — each one changes the maximum size of a working human group.
  • Shared stories let strangers cooperate. Money, nations, companies, gods and laws are all the same move: a fiction enough people act on that it becomes real.
  • Anything that scales cooperation wins, regardless of whether it is true, kind or deserved.

Those four are our subject. Not the events — the levers that produced them.

Who it is for

Aged roughly 25-45, tech or business minded, reads non-fiction to understand the world. Not an ignorant person. Often a well-educated one, which is the sharpest part of the pain: he feels he should already know this, so he will not buy anything that treats him as a beginner or a student.

He reads the news and follows every individual story while understanding none of the situation. He does not want to become a history person. He wants to stop feeling like the world is happening in a language he never learned.

This is evidenced, not guessed. On Reddit, 17 separate threads ask for "a book like Sapiens," and the complaints about what gets recommended are always the same: too dense, too fast, no chance to absorb, bored out of my mind. They are not asking for more history. They are asking for history that explains something and then ends.

Explicitly not for: hardcore history fans (they know the material and will judge us on the detail we deliberately cut) and students or exam-takers (they want exactly the detail we remove).

What we promise

One force per book. What it made possible, what it destroyed, and why the world looks like this because of it. Not a survey. Not a timeline. One lever, explained until the reader can use it on tomorrow's news.

Four promises, honest, repeated everywhere:

  1. One idea per book. Not all of human history at once.
  2. No dates to memorise. If a name or a year is not doing work, it is cut.
  3. Mechanism, not events. You leave able to explain something, not able to recite it.
  4. You will finish it. Short by design — an evening, not a season.

Promise 3 is the new one and it is the whole product. The test for every chapter: could the reader now explain this to someone else without looking anything up?

How we signal the Harari promise without saying his name

We never write "reads like Harari" as our own claim. We do not need to — the readers asking for him described what they want in their own words, and those words are free:

  • "Big picture, not a list of events."
  • "Explains why it mattered, not when it happened."
  • "Makes the world make sense."
  • "I actually finished it."

We borrow the reader's vocabulary, not the author's. Where Harari's argument is itself the hook, the rule from notes/harari-ip.md applies: paraphrase, name him, then answer him. Never a verbatim quote on a card, never his name in any Amazon KDP metadata field.

The faceless problem, and how it is resolved

Every history newsletter with real scale is a named human. No faceless brand ever built a large one. That reads like a conflict with the faceless rule; it is not. It is a channel constraint that was mis-attributed to the whole business.

Faceless newsletters fail because a newsletter is a relationship with a person. Faceless short-form image and video is a different medium, and it works at multi-million scale in exactly our niche.

So: the imprint is faceless, and we build on Instagram, not on a personal newsletter. The email list is the destination, not the brand — people join it to get the next book, not to hear from a person.

Why us

Two structural reasons, nothing about skill:

  1. Matt is reader number one. Exact need, no tolerance for boring. If a book bores him it ships to nobody.
  2. We can produce at a cadence a person cannot. Every dead operator in the graveyard died quietly of burnout. A one-person imprint shipping short books on a schedule plus daily cards is only possible because agents do the volume.

What we refuse to be

  • Not a personality. No face, no pen name, no "hi guys."
  • Not a trivia channel. "10 facts you didn't know" is the opposite of mechanism, and that audience does not buy books.
  • Not comprehensive. Wrong-by-omission on purpose, and we say so.
  • Not for the expert. If a specialist is annoyed by what we cut, we cut right.
  • Not free forever. The ad-funded and grant-funded projects in the graveyard all died when the funder left. Readers pay, so readers keep us alive.

Three angles named and rejected

1. "The Sapiens shelf" — another big-picture history of everything. Rejected. Amazon's World History top 20 is 100% traditionally published, zero indie. We would be the four-hundredth book competing on the one axis a solo imprint cannot win. Our edge is the lever and the hook, not the format.

2. "History for hardcore fans" — depth, rigour, the Fall of Civilizations crowd. Rejected, and it is the tempting one: 100M+ downloads, 3,920 paying Patreon members, 45,000 paying subscribers at The Rest Is History. But that audience buys depth and voice, which need years and a named human, and they are the readers most likely to punish what we cut.

3. "History as a curriculum you finish." Rejected as the opening angle, kept as the destination — and this version moves it closer. Because the subject is now mechanism rather than events, the books stop being a shelf of separate topics and become one explanation delivered in parts: tools, then communication, then stories, then power. That is a curriculum whether we call it one or not. We still do not sell class one of a course nobody has heard of, so books ship first and the "read them in order" framing arrives with the catalogue. See catalogue in the queue.

What this commits us to next

  • Every title in the catalogue must name a force, not a period or an event. If a proposed book cannot finish the sentence "this is how X let humans do something they could not do before," it is not our book.
  • The channel is faceless Instagram, feeding an email list the imprint owns.
  • The first paid thing is short and priced for impulse; the bundle is the hero.

Those carry into distribution.md, offer.md and the catalogue.

The one thing this rests on that we have not proved

Google Trends and every keyword-volume tool were blocked, so we have no search-volume validation for any specific topic. The positioning does not depend on it; the topic order does. Close that before locking book one.
